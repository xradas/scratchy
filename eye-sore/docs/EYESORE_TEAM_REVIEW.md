# Eyesore: specialist team and first playable slice

Status: proposal for user review, 2 October 2026. This review round researched Doom and the existing project. It authorizes no further game implementation or asset production by itself. Earlier experimental code and assets remain in the working branch; compilation has not established their artistic quality.

The proposed target is an original fast, readable shooter built around movement, distinct weapon and monster roles, exploration, and convincing audiovisual feedback. Use 1993 Doom as the baseline reference. Label Doom II influences and modern additions explicitly: the published Linux source contains features from more than one release.

## What the research changes

1. **Design connected places.** The current prototype is a rectangle divided into rooms with three globally staged fights. The earlier level brief also relies heavily on killing every enemy to open the next route. Recommend ordinary connected routes, retreat choices, keys, switches, shortcuts and optional exploration. A deliberate arena lock can be a set piece; it should not define every room. This is our design recommendation, informed by Doom's interaction systems, rather than a claim that every Doom map follows one formula.
2. **Teach stable enemy roles.** Doom's actor definitions have archetype health; map skill flags, supplies and special difficulty behavior alter the challenge. Start with consistent health per recognizable creature. Increase pressure through combinations, elevation, cover, distance and resources. A durable variant should look and behave differently. [Actor definitions](https://github.com/id-Software/DOOM/blob/master/linuxdoom-1.10/info.c), [skill-dependent map spawning](https://github.com/id-Software/DOOM/blob/master/linuxdoom-1.10/p_mobj.c).
3. **Make weapons mechanically distinct.** Doom's pistol and shotgun use line attacks; its original shotgun fires seven pellets. Eyesore currently presents pistol/shotgun fire as traveling projectiles. That changes aiming and feedback substantially. Review hitscan versus projectile behavior and the complete fire/recovery cycle before commissioning final animations. The super shotgun belongs to Doom II. [Weapon implementation](https://github.com/id-Software/DOOM/blob/master/linuxdoom-1.10/p_pspr.c).
4. **Set movement before sizing rooms.** Doom applies thrust to momentum and has separate camera/weapon bob behavior. Movement, friction, turn response, aim assistance, actor dimensions and door widths need a shared specification. Compare time to cross a room and time available to dodge; do not transplant raw map units into Eyesore's different scale. [Player movement](https://github.com/id-Software/DOOM/blob/master/linuxdoom-1.10/p_user.c).
5. **Give lighting a real owner.** Doom sectors have brightness and floor/ceiling heights; lighting behaviors include flicker, strobes and glow. Eyesore's flat-height room and largely uniform warm lighting limit both spatial variety and readability. A lighting plan must guide the eye and preserve threats at gameplay distances. [Sector structures](https://github.com/id-Software/DOOM/blob/master/linuxdoom-1.10/r_defs.h), [light behaviors](https://github.com/id-Software/DOOM/blob/master/linuxdoom-1.10/p_lights.c).
6. **Judge sprites in motion and in context.** Doom supports directional, mirrored and single-view frames, offsets, clipping, sector/distance lighting and selected fullbright frames. Eyesore's 384×256 export canvas is a storage contract, not an artistic resolution target. Its current enemy rendering disables lighting and draws health bars: both require a deliberate design decision. [Sprite renderer](https://github.com/id-Software/DOOM/blob/master/linuxdoom-1.10/r_things.c).
7. **Separate sound creation, composition and playback.** Current weapon recipes combine recorded attacks with synthetic thumps/noise and compression. The current 'music' builder explicitly makes sparse foley ambience. Neither supplies a reviewed sound identity or authored score. The recent callback mixer is a prototype: summed peaks hard-clip, pan is fixed at voice start, and music runs through a separate device. Doom's sound manager has channel priorities, positional updates and separate music handling. [Sound management](https://github.com/id-Software/DOOM/blob/master/linuxdoom-1.10/s_sound.c).
8. **Review the whole shot.** Audio attack, muzzle flash, damage/projectile release, recoil, enemy pain and recovery must agree. More bass or a larger sprite cannot establish this alone. Bobby Prince describes working against the animation and iterating its sounds; that is a useful production method. [Composer interview](https://quartercircles.wordpress.com/2020/04/02/bobby-prince-composer-of-doom-doom-2-interview/).

## Proposed specialist roster

These are bounded task roles. We can run three specialist agents alongside the coordinating agent. The same worker can receive a different brief after finishing its current assignment. Each assignment produces a small artifact for review before broad production.

| ID / role | Responsibility | First deliverable to review | Required quality evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 00 Art direction and visual development | Shared visual identity, palette, pixel density, proportions and world/monster/weapon relationships | One-page style guide and one representative combat-scene composition | One coherent visual language; readable targets and pickups; approved on-screen scale before bulk generation |
| 01 Engine architecture and simulation | World representation, simulation timing, collision, renderer capability and performance constraints | Capability matrix comparing the current engine with requirements; a recommendation on extending it versus adopting an established base, including migration cost | One coherent source for rendering/collision/LOS; feasible heights/doors/tools; no engine migration based only on familiarity |
| 02 Player movement and aiming | Acceleration, friction, run/strafe, wall sliding, view height, mouse input, aim assistance and bob | Movement contract and proposed movement-room scenarios; explicit classic aiming/free-look policy | Predictable control across frame rates; dodge room; comfortable camera; adjustable input |
| 03 Weapon mechanics | Weapon roles, spread, damage, ammo cost, cadence, switch/refire/recovery rules | Pistol-like, shotgun-like and melee role cards; event timelines | Each weapon has a purpose; timing is shared with art/SFX; agreed shots-to-kill at useful distances |
| 04 Enemy behavior | Awareness, pursuit, attack commitment, melee/projectiles, pain, death, infighting and collision | Caster, melee pressure and durable anchor behavior cards/state diagrams | Readable tells; attacks can be understood and countered; tougher roles change tactics |
| 05 Encounters, resources and difficulty | Enemy placement, health/armor/ammo budget, acquisition order and difficulty variants | Room encounter sheets with entry resources, lesson, escape routes and expected expenditure | Main route works without secrets; familiar enemies stay predictable; pressure rises through composition |
| 06 Level layout and navigation | Room graph, dimensions, elevation, vistas, loops, landmarks and optional routes | Two original top-down layout alternatives plus a height section for a 5–8 minute level | Explicit connected graph; readable return route; useful retreat/bypass choices; no accidental dead ends |
| 07 World interactions | Doors, keys, switches, lifts, hazards, teleports, secret counting and exit state | Interaction state diagrams and data schema for the required first-level subset | Visual/physical state agrees; restart is complete; progression cannot lose its required key |
| 08 Lighting and visibility | Per-area brightness, light hierarchy, flicker/glow, navigation emphasis and threat contrast | Lighting plan for entry, combat room and dark optional area, with camera compositions | Enemies and pickups read at intended range; navigation survives reduced flashing and colour differences |
| 09 Environment materials and props | Wall/floor/ceiling vocabulary, palette, texel scale, trims, tiling, signs and environmental clues | Small coherent material kit specification and room identity sheet | Consistent scale/alignment; interactables and hazards read clearly; clutter preserves target visibility |
| 10 Creature sprites and animation | Silhouette/anatomy, directional views, walking, casting, pain, death and corpse frames | One caster silhouette study, eight-view contact sheet, then short cast/death previews | Stable feet/scale/anatomy; distinct poses; transparent edges; readable on-screen size in dark/light rooms |
| 11 First-person weapon art and animation | Weapon/hands silhouette, muzzle anchor, idle/fire/recovery/switch presentation | One shotgun-like cycle aligned to the weapon mechanic's event timeline | Stable grip/muzzle; clear recoil and recovery; framing supports aiming |
| 12 Combat effects and feedback | Muzzle flashes, projectile cores, surface/flesh impacts, blood/debris, damage and pickup feedback | One synchronized shot-to-hit sequence specification and effect storyboard | Hit/miss and incoming threats remain readable; bounded flash/shake; effects respect gameplay timing |
| 13 Weapon and impact sound design | Original firing attacks, mechanism sounds, surface/flesh impact families and variants | Three meaningfully different shotgun treatments, each isolated and in repeated fire, matched in loudness | User preference established by listening; identifiable attack/body/tail; no fatigue or masked warnings |
| 14 Creature and environmental sound design | Alert/cast/pain/death identity, footsteps if useful, doors/lifts/keys/pickups, ambient zones | One complete creature sound family plus one door and one ambient sketch | Events carry information without sight; consistent creature identity; ambience does not mimic danger cues |
| 15 Music composition | Original motif, rhythm, harmony, instrumentation, arrangement and loop structure | Two short original musical sketches with editable notes/MIDI/source and an effects mix preview | Memorable direction; deliberate musical development; loop seam; space for combat sounds |
| 16 Audio programming and mix | Concurrent voices, buses, gain/headroom, priorities, spatial updates, attenuation, loop/device handling | Playback/mix contract and proposed controlled audition scenario | No delay/cutoff/clipping faults; moving sources update; separate music/effects controls; real listening review |
| 17 HUD, automap and interface | Health/armor/ammo/keys, weapon state, interaction cues, map disclosure, menus, options and readable feedback | Two original HUD mockups, automap rules and an options outline | Status can be read during combat; key/secret disclosure deliberate; colour is not the sole signal |
| 18 Content tools and asset pipeline | Manifests, import/export, pivots, palettes, naming, previews and designer-friendly editing | Shared asset/event schema and import/preview workflow | Repeatable exports; source provenance; detects missing frames; content changes do not require many code edits |
| 19 Session systems and release | Save/load, restart, progression, configuration, packaging, input focus and Linux launch behavior | First-slice lifecycle diagram and packaging checklist | Clean launch/restart/exit; settings persist; correct assets included; saves have defined scope |
| 20 Independent quality and playtest analysis | Functional checks, performance, balance evidence, visual review and listening comparisons | Short repeatable playtest rubric and defect/feedback log | Measures confusion, deaths, resources, timing and frame pacing; separates bugs from artistic preference |

The coordinating agent owns the shared brief, cross-discipline decisions, source tracking, integration, and the playable handoff. The user supplies the creative direction and final play/listening judgment. Narrative naming and theme are part of that shared brief initially; a dedicated narrative agent becomes useful when there is enough game content to support it.

## Shared contracts before production

- **Reference contract:** 1993-feel movement/combat and sector-style spaces, original Eyesore art/sounds/maps; separately decide free look, autoaim, crosshair, monster health bars, jumping, bob and modern comfort controls. Recommended starting point: levels never require jumping; offer modern input/comfort options; review free look versus classic aiming in the movement prototype.
- **World contract:** units, player/enemy dimensions, step height, collision margins, floor/ceiling openings, sight tests and door state. The existing fixed-height arena does not yet establish this.
- **Weapon event contract:** accepted trigger, ammunition consumption, damage trace or projectile release, muzzle frame, firing sound, recoil, recovery and permitted weapon switch. The weapons agent owns this, with animator/SFX/effects sign-off at review.
- **Creature event contract:** facing/foot pivot, movement timing, wind-up, attack release, pain interrupt, death and final corpse, including which events emit audio.
- **Art contract:** screen-space pixel density, palette, sprite lighting/fullbright rules, material scale, silhouette contrast, transparency and camera framing. Generated sheets remain concepts until frame inspection proves they satisfy it.
  For production sprites, evaluate hand-cleaned keyframes or a consistent model rendered from the required directions and poses, followed by deliberate pixel cleanup. Generating thirty-two independent-looking pictures does not establish one stable animated creature.
- **Audio contract:** cue identity, source/license, variation policy, playback origin, gain/priority, category and looping behavior. Keep headroom; evaluate at matched loudness; preserve an editable source for every chosen design.
- **Level contract:** one editable definition for geometry, encounters, pickups, interactions and exits; an explicit graph and resource budget. Avoid parallel hard-coded lists that can disagree.

## Work order after this plan is reviewed

1. **Agree the reference and foundation.** Engine, movement and art-direction briefs define the constraints. Review whether the current engine can support the intended sector/height/interactions workflow efficiently. No detailed level or full roster should depend on an unmade engine decision.
2. **Prove one combat exchange.** One original room with an entry and return route, one pistol-like weapon, one shotgun-like weapon and one caster. Prepare three sound treatments and two music sketches for listening. Lighting, sprite and weapon-animation previews all use this same encounter. Weapon mechanics, audio attack, animation and enemy reaction must agree.
3. **Build a complete small level.** Target 5–8 minutes on a first successful run, with a key loop, shortcut, one optional secret, height variation and a clear exit. Add a melee pressure enemy; introduce a third role only after the first two are readable. Pistol, shotgun and melee fallback are enough for this milestone; the arc weapon has a later reward opportunity.
4. **Review integrated quality.** Replay with music off/on, on speakers/headphones if available, and across comfort settings. Check near/mid/far enemy readability, corners/doors/lifts, restart and packaging. Use feedback to revise the responsible discipline and repeat only the affected comparisons.
5. **Expand from accepted work.** Extend weapons, enemy roster, maps, original music and environmental variety once the first complete slice establishes a quality bar.

Suggested production batches (maximum three specialists at once): foundation/movement/art brief; weapon mechanics/SFX treatment/layout alternatives; enemy behavior/sprite study/weapon animation; lighting/materials/music; interactions/resources/HUD; audio integration/pipeline/session systems; independent review and focused revisions. Dependencies may require returning to a previous specialist. These are sequencing proposals, not current implementation tasks.

## Review gates and working rules

- Each task brief declares inputs, one bounded output, allowed files, dependencies, acceptance evidence and unresolved decisions. Research separates observed Doom behavior, current Eyesore behavior and proposed changes.
- Use isolated branches/worktrees for implementation after a baseline checkpoint. Asset designers work in their own source/prototype directories. A single integrator controls shared engine interfaces and resolves overlap.
- Retain source assets and provenance. When considering actual reuse of released engine code, evaluate its license separately from game artwork, audio and map content.
- Technical validation checks dimensions, formats, state transitions and behavior. Artistic review checks how it looks, sounds and feels in the actual scene. A clean build or a waveform peak is not artistic approval.
- Audio review compares isolated and repeated firing at matched loudness, then the same clips under combat and music. Ask about weight, identity, clarity and fatigue. The current WAVs have not passed that review.
- Sprite review covers contact sheets plus animation, turning, pain and death at actual gameplay sizes against the room's lighting. The current Kiln Wretch sheet is a concept; its painterly detail, irregular grid and pose consistency require work.
- The first-level brief remains a proposal. Resolve its G2/map-table discrepancy and intake/hub link, remove blanket kill gates, stabilize archetype HP and tie supplies to weapon damage before implementing its map.
- Newly assigned agents are focused general-purpose collaborators. The current tools support code, source research, audio editing/synthesis and image concepts; a dedicated high-quality music-generation workflow has not been demonstrated. Listen to a small sample before promising a complete score. Additional specialist tools or a human composer may become appropriate if prototypes miss the agreed quality bar.
